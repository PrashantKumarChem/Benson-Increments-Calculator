Every group name has the same shape: the central atom, a dash, then the
ligands in brackets with a count where there is more than one.

`C-(C)2(H)2` is a carbon bonded to two carbons and two hydrogens — the
methylene of an alkane chain. `CO-(C)2` is a carbonyl carbon bonded to two
carbons, which is to say a ketone.

## The ligands are not counted twice

A ligand says what the central atom is bonded to. It does **not** contribute
that atom to the molecule. In `C-(C)3(H)`, the three bracketed carbons are
each counted by their own group; counting them here as well would give every
carbon one extra for each neighbour it happens to have.

Hydrogen is the exception, and today the only one. A hydrogen never has a
group of its own, so the group it is written in is the only place it is ever
counted.

## Searching by what you would write

You do not have to type Benson notation to find a group. The calculator reads
the notation, so a carbon with three hydrogen ligands is findable as `CH3`
even though those three characters appear nowhere in `C-(C)(H)3`. Common
names work too — `methyl`, `ketone`, `hydroxyl` — and are listed in
`notation/synonyms.csv`.
